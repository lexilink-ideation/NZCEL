### Audio script

Kia ora koutou, good morning, everyone. Last week we looked at how freshwater ecosystems work: how energy moves from algae and plants to insects, to fish, and to birds. Today I want to look at what happens when a new species arrives in one of these systems and starts to take over. These are what biologists call invasive species. I'm going to organise the lecture into four parts. First, I'll define what an invasive species is. Second, I'll explain how these species travel to new places. Third, we'll look at why some of them become so successful, and what damage they do. And finally, I'll talk about how they can be managed. So you might want to set up your notes with those four headings.

Right, part one: definitions. Not every species that comes from somewhere else is a problem. Biologists use the term introduced species, or non-native species, for any species that has been moved, usually by people, outside the area where it evolved. Many introduced species never establish a population at all. They simply die out. Others establish themselves but stay in small numbers and cause very little harm. An invasive species is the smaller group of introduced species that spread quickly and cause harm, either to the environment, to the economy, or to human health. So the key idea is that invasive means two things together: rapid spread, and harmful effects. Keep that in mind, because people often use the word invasive for any non-native species, and that isn't quite accurate.

Moving on to part two: how do these species get into new rivers and lakes? There are four main pathways I'd like you to note down. The first is shipping. Large ships take in water, called ballast water, to keep them stable, and then release it in another port, sometimes on the other side of the world. That water can carry small animals, larvae and algae. The second pathway, and probably the most important for inland lakes and rivers, is recreational equipment. Boats, trailers, fishing gear, kayaks and even wet boots can carry tiny organisms from one waterway to the next. Some of these organisms can survive for days or even weeks in damp conditions. The third pathway is the aquarium and pond trade. When people empty a home aquarium into a stream, or plants escape from a garden pond, those species may find themselves in a perfect new habitat. And the fourth pathway is deliberate release. In the past, fish were sometimes introduced on purpose, for food or for sport, without much thought about the consequences. So that's shipping, equipment, the aquarium trade, and deliberate release.

Now, part three, which is really the heart of today's lecture. Why do some introduced species become invasive, when most don't? Researchers have identified several features that successful invaders tend to share. The first is that they often arrive without their natural enemies. In its home range, a species is kept under control by predators, parasites and diseases that have evolved alongside it. When it is moved to a new place, those enemies are usually left behind. Some scientists call this the enemy release idea. The second feature is that invaders tend to be generalists. In other words, they can eat many different foods and tolerate a wide range of conditions, such as different water temperatures or levels of pollution. And the third feature is fast reproduction. A species that matures quickly and produces large numbers of offspring can build up a population before anyone notices it is there.

Now, I should add a note of caution here. These features don't guarantee that a species will become invasive, and not every invader has all three. Timing and luck matter as well. A species might be introduced many times and fail, and then succeed on the next attempt because conditions happen to be right, perhaps a warm summer, or a river that has recently been disturbed by flooding. In fact, disturbed or polluted habitats are often easier to invade, because native species there are already under stress. So it's more accurate to say these features increase the risk, rather than predict the outcome.

So what kind of damage do invasive species do once they're established? Again, I'd like you to note three main types of impact. The first is competition. An invader may compete with native species for food or space, and because it has no natural enemies, it often wins. The second is predation. Some invasive fish, for example, eat the eggs and young of native fish, or the insects that native fish depend on. And the third type of impact, which is perhaps less obvious, is changing the habitat itself. Let me give you two examples of this. Some invasive fish feed by stirring up the mud on the bottom of a lake. This makes the water cloudy, so less light reaches the water plants, and the plants die. And without plants, many other species lose their food and shelter. A second example is an alga called didymo, which can form thick mats on the beds of rivers. Those mats cover the stones where many insects normally live, so the insects decline, and the fish that feed on them may decline too. In both cases, the invader doesn't attack other species directly. It changes the environment, and that change affects the whole food web.

Okay, the final part: management. What can we actually do? It's useful to think about management as a series of stages, and the general principle is that the earlier you act, the cheaper and more effective it is. The first stage, and the most important, is prevention: stopping species from arriving in the first place. This includes rules about ballast water, and campaigns that ask people to clean their boats and gear. Here in New Zealand, you may have seen the message Check, Clean, Dry, which asks people to check their equipment for plant material, clean it, and dry it completely before moving to another waterway. The second stage is early detection. If a new species is found while its population is still small, it may be possible to remove it completely. Early detection depends on people actually looking, and that's where regular monitoring comes in. Scientists sample water and check sites that are visited often, such as boat ramps. Increasingly, members of the public help too, by reporting anything unusual they see when they're fishing or tramping. This kind of community involvement, sometimes called citizen science, means many more eyes on many more waterways than scientists could ever cover alone. That's the third stage, eradication. Unfortunately, once a species has spread widely, eradication usually becomes impossible, or far too expensive. At that point, we move to the fourth stage, which is long-term control. This means keeping numbers low enough to limit the damage, for example by trapping or fishing out invasive fish, or by protecting the most valuable sites. Control can work, but it never really ends, and it has to be paid for year after year.

So, let me summarise. An invasive species is an introduced species that spreads quickly and causes harm. They arrive through four main pathways: shipping, recreational equipment, the aquarium trade, and deliberate release. They succeed because they often escape their natural enemies, they are generalists, and they reproduce quickly. They cause harm through competition, predation and changing the habitat. And management moves through prevention, early detection, eradication and long-term control, with prevention being by far the most cost-effective. That's why, in my view, the most useful thing any of us can do is the simplest: clean your gear before you move between rivers. Next week we'll look at a case study of one lake restoration project. Thanks, everyone.

### Audio script

Moderator: Good evening, and welcome to tonight's panel. Our question is a simple one, but the answers are not: if we want to reduce food waste, where should we focus our efforts? On farms, in shops and supermarkets, or in our own homes? I'm joined by three people who look at this from very different angles. Dr Grace Lin researches food supply chains at the university. Mr Tom Brooks manages sustainability for a group of supermarkets. And Ms Anika Patel runs a community programme that teaches cooking and food-saving skills. Dr Lin, let's start with you. Where is food actually lost?

Dr Lin: Thank you. The first thing I always say is that food is lost at every stage, from the farm to the plate. On farms, some crops are never harvested, because prices fall too low and it isn't worth paying people to pick them. Other produce is rejected because it's the wrong shape or size for what buyers want. Then during storage and transport, food can spoil, especially fresh produce, if it isn't kept cold. In lower-income countries, a lot of the loss happens at these early stages, mainly because of poor storage and transport. But in wealthier countries like ours, research consistently suggests that a large share of waste happens at the end of the chain, in households. So my answer is that it depends on the country. For us, I'd say homes matter a great deal, but we shouldn't ignore the farm.

Moderator: Mr Brooks, supermarkets are often blamed for food waste. Is that fair?

Mr Brooks: Partly fair, I'd say. I don't want to pretend we're perfect. But I'd make two points. First, supermarkets themselves usually throw away a smaller share of food than households do. That's because waste costs us money directly, so we have a strong reason to reduce it. We use better forecasting to order the right amounts, and we sell food close to its date at a discount, or donate it to food rescue charities. Second, and this is where I agree with the critics, supermarkets have a big influence on waste that happens elsewhere. Our standards for how fruit and vegetables should look can lead to waste on farms. And our promotions, like buy two, get one free, can encourage customers to buy more than they can eat. So my view is that retailers should focus less on their own bins and more on their influence: changing cosmetic standards, and rethinking promotions.

Moderator: That's an interesting admission. Ms Patel, you work with households. Do you agree that homes are where the biggest gains are?

Ms Patel: I do, but I want to change the way we talk about it. When people hear that households waste a lot of food, they often think it's because people are careless. In my experience, that's rarely true. Most people hate wasting food. The problem is usually a lack of skills and time. People don't plan meals, so they buy too much. They don't know how to store food so it lasts longer. And many are confused by date labels. A best-before date is about quality, not safety. Food is often perfectly fine to eat after that date. A use-by date is different; that one is about safety. In our programme, once people learn these simple things, their waste usually goes down quickly. So yes, I'd focus on households, but through education and practical support, not by blaming people.

Moderator: Dr Lin, is education enough?

Dr Lin: I don't think education alone is enough, and I think Ms Patel would agree. Research on behaviour change shows that information on its own often has a limited effect. People need the right environment too. For example, clearer date labels, smaller pack sizes for people who live alone, and better food storage at home. That's why I don't think we should choose just one stage. The stages are connected. If supermarkets change their promotions, households buy less. If labels are clearer, households throw away less. The most effective approach is probably a combination.

Mr Brooks: I'd agree with that, and I'd add one thing. Retailers are in the middle of the chain, so we're actually in a good position to connect the other two. We talk to farmers, and we talk to customers every day.

Ms Patel: That's true, though I'd say communities need to be part of it as well. Change happens more easily when people learn together.

Moderator: We have time for one question from the audience. Yes, the person at the front.

Audience member: Thanks. My question is about food that's already wasted. Isn't composting it a good solution? Then it isn't really wasted.

Ms Patel: That's a good question, and composting is certainly better than sending food to landfill. But I'd say it should be the last option, not the first. When food is composted, all the water, energy and work that went into growing, transporting and cooking it are still lost. So the priority should be not to waste it in the first place. Then, if there's surplus, give it to people who can eat it. And only then compost what's left.

Mr Brooks: I'd agree. In our stores we think of it as a ladder. At the top is preventing surplus by ordering better. Next is redistributing food to charities. Composting and similar options come near the bottom. Each step down the ladder recovers less of the original value.

Dr Lin: And from a research point of view, that ladder idea is widely used. It's a helpful way to compare options, because it reminds us that prevention almost always brings the biggest benefit.

Moderator: So, to sum up, it sounds as though all three of you see value in each stage, but with different emphasis. Dr Lin, you'd say it depends on the country, and that a combined approach works best. Mr Brooks, you'd focus on how retailers influence both farms and customers. And Ms Patel, you'd focus on households, through practical skills rather than blame. Thank you all very much.

### Small Loans, Big Expectations: An Introduction to Microfinance

#### 1. What is microfinance?

[1] Around the world, many people who want to start or grow a small business face the same problem: they cannot borrow money. Banks are often unwilling to lend to them for several reasons. The loans they need are very small, so the profit for the bank is low compared with the cost of processing each application. Many of these borrowers have no formal credit history, so the bank cannot easily judge whether they will repay. And most have no collateral, which is property such as land or a house that a bank can take if a loan is not repaid. As a result, people on low incomes have often had to rely on informal moneylenders, who may charge very high interest rates. Borrowing from such lenders can trap families in a cycle of debt, in which a large part of any income goes on interest rather than on improving the business.

[2] Microfinance is the general name for financial services designed for people who are excluded from ordinary banking. The best-known service is microcredit, which means very small loans, often used to buy materials or equipment for a small business such as a market stall, a tailoring shop or a small farm. However, microfinance also includes savings accounts, insurance and money transfer services. In this section, we focus mainly on microcredit, because it has received the most attention, both positive and negative. It is worth noting, however, that the different services are closely connected. A family that can save safely may need to borrow less, and a family with insurance may be less likely to fall into debt after an emergency.

#### 2. How group lending works

[3] Modern microcredit is closely associated with the economist Muhammad Yunus and the Grameen Bank, which he founded in Bangladesh. In 2006, Yunus and the Grameen Bank were jointly awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for their work. The Grameen model, and many others based on it, used an approach known as group lending. Although the details vary between organisations and countries, the basic principles of this approach have been copied by lenders in many parts of Asia, Africa and Latin America.

[4] In group lending, borrowers form small groups, often of about five people, who usually live in the same village or neighbourhood. Each member receives an individual loan, but the members share responsibility for repayment. If one member does not repay, the others may be required to cover the debt, or the whole group may lose access to future loans. This arrangement is known as joint liability.

[5] Joint liability is designed to solve the problems that make lending to the poor difficult. First, because group members know each other well, they are in a better position than a bank to judge who is likely to repay. People are unlikely to form a group with someone they consider unreliable. Second, members have a reason to monitor each other and to encourage repayment, because they share the risk. This pressure from neighbours is sometimes called social collateral: it replaces the physical collateral that poor borrowers do not have. Third, group meetings, which are usually held weekly, reduce the lender's costs, because a single loan officer can meet many borrowers at once.

[6] Another feature of many microcredit programmes is the repayment schedule. Instead of repaying the whole loan at the end, borrowers usually begin making small, regular repayments, often weekly, soon after receiving the loan. Supporters argue that this builds financial discipline and keeps debts manageable. Critics, however, point out that it can be difficult for businesses that earn most of their income at one time of year, such as farms that depend on a single harvest.

#### 3. Why expectations were high

[7] During the 1990s and early 2000s, microcredit became extremely popular with governments, charities and international organisations. Repayment rates reported by many lenders were very high, which seemed to show that poor borrowers were reliable and that lending to them could be financially sustainable. Many stories described individual borrowers, often women, who had used a small loan to build a successful business, send their children to school and improve their family's living conditions.

[8] Supporters made a further argument. Because many microcredit programmes lent mainly to women, they suggested that microcredit could increase women's control over household decisions as well as their income. If this were true, microcredit would be not only an economic tool but also a way of improving gender equality. For these reasons, microcredit was widely presented as one of the most promising ways of reducing poverty. The idea was also attractive because it seemed to offer a way of helping people that relied on their own effort and initiative, rather than on charity. In addition, if lenders could cover their costs from interest payments, programmes could continue and grow without depending on donations.

#### 4. What the evidence shows

[9] Individual success stories are powerful, but they cannot tell us whether microcredit works on average. The people who choose to take loans may be different from those who do not. For example, they may already be more confident, more experienced in business or better connected. If so, their later success might have happened even without a loan. To answer the question properly, researchers need to compare similar groups of people, some of whom are offered microcredit and some of whom are not.

[10] From around 2010, several large studies did exactly this. In these studies, carried out in a number of different countries, microcredit was introduced into some randomly selected areas but not into others. Because the areas were chosen randomly, any differences that appeared later could reasonably be linked to the availability of microcredit rather than to differences between the people involved.

[11] The results were more modest than many people had expected. Taken together, the studies found that access to microcredit did help some households to start or expand businesses, and it gave families more flexibility in how they managed their money. However, on average, the studies did not find large increases in household income, and there was little evidence of major improvements in areas such as health, education or women's decision-making. In other words, microcredit appeared to be useful, but it was not the transformative solution to poverty that it had often been described as. It is important to note that the studies did not find that microcredit was harmful on average, and some households clearly benefited. The main conclusion was that the benefits were smaller and less widespread than earlier reports had suggested.

[12] An important finding was that the effects were not the same for everyone. Borrowers who already ran a business before the loan, and who had more business experience, tended to benefit more. For many others, especially those without an existing business, the loans were more often used for household needs, such as buying food or paying for unexpected expenses, than for investment.

#### 5. Criticisms and risks

[13] As the evidence became clearer, critics also drew attention to several risks. The first concerns interest rates. Because small loans are expensive to manage, microfinance institutions often charge higher interest rates than ordinary banks. Lenders argue that these rates are necessary to cover their costs and are still lower than those charged by informal moneylenders. Critics reply that high rates can make it hard for borrowers to earn enough from their businesses to repay.

[14] The second risk is over-indebtedness. In some regions, the number of lenders grew very quickly, and some borrowers took loans from several lenders at the same time, sometimes using one loan to repay another. When this happens, debts can build up until borrowers are unable to repay any of them. The third criticism concerns the social pressure created by joint liability. The same pressure that encourages repayment can also cause stress and conflict within communities if a member falls behind.

#### 6. Beyond loans

[15] Partly in response to these findings, many microfinance organisations have broadened their services. Instead of focusing only on credit, they increasingly offer safe ways to save, insurance against events such as illness or crop failure, and digital payment services, which allow money to be sent and received by mobile phone. Some researchers argue that for many poor households, a safe place to save may be at least as valuable as a loan, because it allows them to build up money gradually without the risk of debt. Digital services may also reduce costs, because loans can be paid out and repaid through a phone rather than through weekly meetings. Whether these newer approaches will prove more effective than traditional microcredit is a question that researchers are still investigating.

#### 7. Conclusion

[16] Microcredit began with a simple and attractive idea: that small loans could allow poor people to build businesses and escape poverty. The group lending model offered a clever solution to the problems that had prevented banks from lending to the poor. However, careful research suggests that, on average, its effects are positive but modest, and that it brings real risks if it is poorly managed. The current view of many economists is that microfinance is a useful tool, especially when it includes savings and insurance as well as credit, but that it cannot by itself solve the complex problem of poverty. Improvements in education, health, infrastructure and employment are also needed, and the story of microcredit shows why it is important to test even the most attractive ideas carefully before treating them as a solution.

## L4-R2 · Research article: Tab-switching and online lectures

L4-08 · short research article (simplified) · T405 Attention and online learning · short research article (simplified version)

> **Check before release:** Study is hypothetical and labelled as such. Background claims about divided attention and retrieval practice are kept general.

### Does Switching Tabs Reduce Learning from Online Lectures? A Hypothetical Study

#### Abstract

[1] Many university students now watch lectures online, often while other websites and applications are open on the same device. This hypothetical study examined whether switching between browser tabs during a recorded lecture affects how much students learn. One hundred and twenty first-year students watched the same 30-minute lecture and then completed a test. Half were asked to keep only the lecture open; the other half watched under their normal conditions. Students in the normal-conditions group switched tabs frequently and scored lower on the test, particularly on questions that required them to connect ideas. The findings suggest that the flexibility of online learning comes with a cost to attention.

#### Background

[2] Online lectures offer students clear advantages. They can be watched at any time, paused and replayed. However, they also take place on devices that offer constant distractions, such as messages, social media and news. Earlier research on attention suggests that people cannot fully concentrate on two demanding tasks at once. When we appear to multitask, we are usually switching quickly between tasks, and each switch has a small cost: it takes time to refocus, and some information may be missed. These costs may seem small individually, but over the course of a long lecture they could add up.

[3] Most research on this topic has been carried out in physical classrooms, for example by studying students who use laptops during face-to-face lectures. Less is known about online lectures, where there is no teacher in the room and no classmates to notice what a student is doing. This study therefore aimed to measure how often students switch tabs during an online lecture, and whether this behaviour is related to learning.

#### Method

[4] The participants were 120 first-year students from a range of subjects. They were randomly divided into two groups of 60. All students watched the same recorded lecture on an introductory psychology topic that none of them had studied before. Students in the focused group were asked to close all other tabs and applications and to keep their phones in another room. Students in the normal group received no instructions about other tabs or devices; they were simply asked to watch the lecture as they usually would. The two groups were similar in age, subject of study and previous grades, which made it more likely that any differences in results were caused by the viewing conditions.

[5] With the students' permission, a browser extension recorded how many times each student moved away from the lecture tab, and for how long. Immediately after the lecture, all students completed a 20-question test. Ten questions tested recall of individual facts, such as definitions. The other ten tested understanding, for example by asking students to explain how two ideas from different parts of the lecture were connected.

#### Findings

[6] Students in the normal group switched away from the lecture tab many times, on average more than once every two minutes, although most visits to other tabs were short. Students in the focused group rarely switched.

[7] On the test, the focused group achieved higher scores overall. However, the difference was not the same for both types of question. On the recall questions, the two groups performed fairly similarly. The larger difference appeared on the understanding questions, where the focused group performed noticeably better. Within the normal group, students who switched tabs more often tended to have lower scores on these questions.

[8] Interestingly, when students were asked afterwards how much they thought they had learned, the two groups gave similar answers. In other words, students who had switched tabs frequently did not seem to be aware that it had affected their learning.

#### Discussion

[9] The results suggest that tab-switching has a greater effect on deeper understanding than on remembering individual facts. One possible explanation is that connecting ideas requires students to hold earlier points in mind while following new ones. Each switch to another tab may interrupt this process, so that students remember separate facts but miss how they fit together.

[10] The finding that students did not notice the effect is also important. If learners believe they can multitask without cost, they have little reason to change their behaviour. Teachers and universities may therefore need to make this cost more visible, for example by sharing findings like these with students, or by designing online lectures with short sections and regular questions that encourage students to stay focused. Students themselves could also use simple strategies, such as closing other applications before starting a lecture or setting aside specific times to check messages.

#### Limitations

[11] This study has several limitations. First, it used only one lecture on one topic, so the results may not apply to all subjects or to longer courses. Second, the test was taken immediately after the lecture, so the study cannot show whether the differences would last. Third, although students agreed to have their tab use recorded, knowing that they were being observed may have changed their behaviour. Future research should follow students over a whole course and measure learning at several points in time.

### Source A: Why are cities hotter than the countryside?
_Introductory urban geography text (adapted)_

Many cities are noticeably warmer than the rural areas around them, especially at night. This is known as the urban heat island effect. There are three main causes. First, materials such as asphalt, concrete and brick absorb heat from the sun during the day and release it slowly after dark. Second, cities have fewer plants. In rural areas, trees and grass release water vapour through their leaves, a process called evapotranspiration, which cools the surrounding air. Third, buildings, vehicles and air-conditioning units produce waste heat.

Urban planners have proposed several responses. The most common is to increase green space: planting street trees, creating parks and building green roofs. Trees also provide shade, which lowers the temperature of surfaces such as roads and walls. A second approach is to use lighter-coloured materials for roofs and pavements, because light surfaces reflect more sunlight than dark ones.

However, these solutions are not equally effective everywhere. In dry climates, plants need irrigation, and water may be scarce. New parks can also increase property values nearby, which may push lower-income residents out of the neighbourhoods the parks were meant to help.
