### Audio script

Good evening, and thank you for joining this webinar. My name is Dr Helen Marsh, and I teach in the School of Population Health. Tonight's topic is antimicrobial resistance, which is often shortened to AMR. My aim is twofold. First, I want to explain, in plain terms, what resistance actually is and how it develops. And second, I want to make an argument: that we've been framing AMR mainly as a problem of individual behaviour, when it's better understood as a problem of systems and incentives. I'll finish with what that means for policy.

Let's start with terminology, because it causes a lot of confusion. Antimicrobials are medicines that kill or slow the growth of microorganisms. Antibiotics, which act on bacteria, are the best-known group, but the term also covers antivirals, antifungals and antiparasitic drugs. Resistance means that a microorganism is no longer affected by a medicine that used to work against it. And here's the first common misunderstanding I'd like to correct. It's not people who become resistant to antibiotics. It's the bacteria. A patient who has taken many courses of antibiotics hasn't built up immunity to the drug; rather, the bacteria in and around that patient may have changed. This matters because the language we use shapes the solutions we look for. If we think the problem lies in the patient's body, we focus on the individual. If we understand that the problem lies in bacterial populations, which are shared across a whole community, we start to see AMR as a collective issue.

So how does that change happen? The underlying mechanism is natural selection. In any large population of bacteria, there's variation. A small number may, by chance, carry a genetic mutation that allows them to survive a particular drug, for instance by pumping the drug out of the cell, or by altering the target the drug normally attacks. When an antibiotic is used, the susceptible bacteria die, but the resistant ones survive and multiply. Over time, the resistant type becomes dominant. What makes bacteria particularly concerning is that they can also share resistance genes with one another, even across different species, through a process called horizontal gene transfer. In effect, a useful survival trick can spread through a bacterial community without each organism having to evolve it independently.

It follows that every use of an antibiotic creates what we call selection pressure. That doesn't mean we shouldn't use antibiotics. When someone has a serious bacterial infection, treatment can save their life. The problem is unnecessary or inappropriate use: antibiotics taken for viral infections such as colds, where they have no effect; doses that are too low or courses that are stopped in the wrong way; and very large-scale use in animal farming, sometimes to promote growth rather than to treat disease. Resistant bacteria then spread through hospitals, communities, food, water and international travel. Hospitals are a particular concern, because they bring together people who are already unwell, frequent antibiotic use and close contact between patients and staff. In that environment, a resistant strain that appears in one ward can move to another surprisingly quickly unless infection control is strict.

Now, why does this matter so much? Because modern medicine depends on effective antibiotics far more than most people realise. It's not only about treating infections like pneumonia. Routine surgery, caesarean deliveries, cancer chemotherapy and organ transplants all rely on our ability to prevent or treat infections. If antibiotics stop working, procedures we currently consider safe could become far more dangerous. That's why international health organisations have described AMR as one of the most serious threats to global health. It's also a slow-moving threat, and that makes it politically awkward. Unlike a sudden outbreak, it rarely produces dramatic headlines. The costs build up gradually, in longer hospital stays, more expensive second-line treatments and infections that become harder to treat, so it's easy for decision-makers to postpone action.

This brings me to my central argument. Public campaigns about AMR have tended to focus on individuals: patients shouldn't demand antibiotics, and should finish their prescriptions as instructed. Those messages aren't wrong, but I'd suggest they put the weight of responsibility in the wrong place. Consider the incentives facing different groups. A busy doctor with a worried patient may find it quicker and safer, from their own point of view, to prescribe an antibiotic just in case. A farmer may rely on antibiotics to keep animals healthy in crowded conditions. And pharmaceutical companies have very little financial reason to develop new antibiotics, because a new antibiotic, if it's used responsibly, will be kept in reserve and sold in small quantities. In other words, each actor is behaving reasonably within the system they're in, and yet together they produce a harmful outcome. Economists would describe resistance as a negative externality, a cost that falls on others and on the future rather than on the person making the decision. A useful comparison is air pollution. One car journey makes almost no difference, so no individual driver has a strong reason to change. But millions of journeys together create a serious problem. Antibiotic use works in a similar way: each prescription adds a tiny amount of selection pressure, and the cost is shared by everyone.

I should acknowledge the counter-argument. Some colleagues argue that individual behaviour change is the most realistic lever we have, because system change is slow and politically difficult. That's a fair point, and I'm not suggesting we abandon public education. But the evidence suggests that the most effective interventions so far have been systemic: antibiotic stewardship programmes in hospitals, where specialist teams review prescribing; faster diagnostic tests that tell doctors whether an infection is bacterial at all; and regulations that restrict the use of certain antibiotics in agriculture. What these approaches have in common is that they change the conditions under which decisions are made, rather than simply asking people to make better choices. A stewardship team, for example, gives a doctor expert support at the moment of prescribing, which is far more powerful than a poster in the waiting room.

So what would a systems approach look like in practice? I'd highlight three priorities. First, diagnostics: if we can identify the cause of an infection quickly, doctors don't need to prescribe just in case. Second, surveillance: we need reliable data on which resistant organisms are circulating, and where, so that we can respond early. And third, new economic models for antibiotic development. One idea that's been discussed internationally is to pay companies a fixed amount for access to a new antibiotic, rather than paying according to how much of it is sold. That way, companies can earn a return even if the drug is rarely used, which is exactly what we want. Some countries have begun experimenting with schemes of this kind. It's too early to say how well they'll work, and they raise difficult questions about cost and about how to share responsibility between countries. But they show that the incentive problem I described isn't impossible to solve.

To sum up, resistance is a natural evolutionary process, but the speed at which it's happening is driven by human systems. Individual responsibility matters, but if we rely on it alone, we're asking people to act against the incentives they face every day. In my view, the real question isn't whether patients will change; it's whether we're willing to redesign the systems around them. I'm happy to take questions now.

## L5-L2 · Podcast: Can algorithms be fair?

L5-04 · academic podcast · T502 Algorithmic decision-making and fairness · podcast

_You will hear an episode of a university podcast about ideas in technology and society. Two hosts talk with a guest researcher about whether automated decisions can be fair. You will hear the episode twice._

> **Check before release:** All people are fictional. The general points about biased training data, proxies and competing definitions of fairness are mainstream; keep them general.

### Audio script

Sam: Welcome back to Thinking Machines, the podcast about ideas in technology and society. I'm Sam.

Priya: And I'm Priya. Today we're asking a question that sounds simple but really isn't: can an algorithm be fair? Algorithms are now used to help make decisions about people: who gets a loan, which job applications are shortlisted, even how public services are allocated. Our guest is Dr Daniel Okafor, who researches the ethics of automated decision-making. Welcome, Daniel.

Dr Okafor: Thanks for having me.

Sam: Let's start with the basics. When people say an algorithm is biased, what do they actually mean? Isn't a computer, by definition, neutral?

Dr Okafor: That's probably the most common assumption, and it's understandable. A computer doesn't have prejudices in the way a person might. But most of the systems we're talking about are built using machine learning, which means they learn patterns from historical data. And if that historical data reflects unequal treatment, the system can learn to reproduce it. A frequently discussed hypothetical is a hiring tool trained on a company's past hiring decisions. If the company mostly hired men in the past, the system may learn that features associated with men predict success, and rank women lower, even though nobody programmed it to do that. The system is simply doing what it was designed to do, which is to find patterns that predicted past outcomes. The trouble is that past outcomes aren't necessarily the outcomes we want.

Priya: So the bias comes from the data rather than the code.

Dr Okafor: Largely, yes, though design choices matter too. And there's a subtler problem, which is what we call proxies. Suppose you remove gender or ethnicity from the data entirely, to make the system blind to them. It seems like a good solution. But other variables can be closely related to those characteristics. A postcode, for example, may reflect patterns of where different communities live. So the system can end up treating groups differently through those proxies, even though the sensitive information itself has been removed. So, somewhat counter-intuitively, making a system blind to a characteristic doesn't guarantee that it treats groups equally. In some cases, you actually need the sensitive information in order to check whether outcomes are fair.

Sam: That's quite worrying. So what does fairness even mean in this context? Can't we just define it and build it in?

Dr Okafor: This is where it gets genuinely difficult, and frankly, it's the part I find most interesting. There isn't one agreed definition of fairness. Let me give you two. One definition says a system is fair if it approves the same proportion of people from each group. Another says it's fair if, when it predicts that someone will repay a loan, that prediction is equally accurate for every group. Both sound reasonable. But researchers have shown that, in many realistic situations, it's mathematically impossible to satisfy both at the same time, particularly when the groups differ in their underlying rates in the historical data.

Priya: So choosing a definition is actually a choice about values.

Dr Okafor: Exactly. And that's my main argument. These decisions are often presented as technical problems for engineers to optimise. But deciding which kind of fairness matters most in a particular context, say lending compared with criminal justice, is an ethical and political decision. It should involve the people affected, not just the people who build the system.

Priya: That's interesting, because it means the same system could be judged fair or unfair depending on which definition you start with.

Dr Okafor: Precisely. Two people can look at the same results and reach opposite conclusions, and both can be using the word fair correctly.

Sam: Let me push back a little. Human decision-makers are biased too. Loan officers and hiring managers make inconsistent judgements, and they can be influenced by all sorts of irrelevant things. Couldn't an algorithm still be an improvement, even if it isn't perfect?

Dr Okafor: That's an important point, and I don't dismiss it. In some situations, a well-designed system may well be more consistent than people are. The comparison shouldn't be between a flawed algorithm and a perfect human; it should be between realistic alternatives. Where I'd be more cautious is about scale and transparency. When one person makes a biased decision, it affects the people in front of them. When an automated system is biased, the same pattern can be repeated across thousands of decisions very quickly, and the people affected may not even know that an algorithm was involved, let alone how to challenge it. There's also a risk of what's sometimes called automation bias: people tend to trust a recommendation more when it comes from a computer, so a human reviewer may simply accept the system's output rather than questioning it.

Sam: Can I ask about something listeners often raise, which is the idea of the black box? People say we can't even see how these systems reach their decisions.

Dr Okafor: That's a real issue, particularly with very complex models. Some systems involve so many variables, interacting in such complicated ways, that even the people who built them can't easily explain why a particular decision was made. Researchers are working on methods that make these systems more explainable, for example by showing which factors had the greatest influence on a decision. But there's often a trade-off. Simpler models are easier to explain, while more complex ones may be slightly more accurate. In high-stakes areas, I'd argue that being able to explain a decision should sometimes matter more than a small gain in accuracy.

Priya: Which brings us to accountability. If a decision goes wrong, who's responsible?

Dr Okafor: That's the question regulators are now grappling with. I'd suggest three principles. First, transparency: people should be told when an automated system has played a significant role in a decision about them. Second, the right to an explanation and to a human review, especially for decisions with serious consequences. And third, regular auditing: organisations should test their systems for unequal outcomes, not just once before launch, but on an ongoing basis, because patterns in data change over time.

Priya: And for our listeners, many of whom are students, what can ordinary people do? It all sounds quite out of our hands.

Dr Okafor: More than you might think. If you're affected by a decision, you can ask whether an automated system was involved and how to request a review. And for students in particular, these questions aren't only for computer scientists. People studying law, sociology, education or public policy all have something to contribute, because the hardest questions here are about values, rights and institutions. Some of the most useful work I've seen has come from teams that bring those perspectives together with technical expertise.

Sam: Are you optimistic, overall?

Dr Okafor: Cautiously, I'd say. I don't think the answer is to ban automated decision-making altogether. That would throw away real benefits. Used carefully, these systems can process applications faster, reduce some kinds of inconsistency, and even help organisations notice unfair patterns in their own past decisions that they hadn't been aware of. But I'm not convinced that fairness can be achieved by technical fixes alone. In the end, the question isn't only whether an algorithm is accurate. It's whether the decisions it supports are ones a society can justify to the people they affect.

Priya: That's a great place to stop. Daniel Okafor, thank you very much.

Dr Okafor: Thank you both.

Sam: Thanks for listening to Thinking Machines. Join us next time.

L5-06 · research paper/report (exit level) · T506 Measuring carbon: accounting methods and their limits · research paper/report

> **Check before release:** The three 'scopes' and the location-based vs market-based methods for electricity are standard features of corporate greenhouse gas accounting; confirm the plain-language descriptions with a reviewer. The study and organisations are fictional.

### Same Organisation, Different Footprint: How Accounting Choices Shape Reported Carbon Emissions (A Hypothetical Study)

#### Abstract

[1] Organisations increasingly publish figures for their greenhouse gas emissions, often called their carbon footprint, and many set targets to reduce them. However, the same organisation can report very different figures depending on the accounting choices it makes. This hypothetical study recalculated the footprints of twelve fictional medium-sized organisations using different, widely accepted methods. The choice of method for electricity emissions changed reported totals considerably, and decisions about which indirect emissions to include changed them even more. We conclude that headline footprint figures should be interpreted with caution and that comparisons between organisations are only meaningful when methods are clearly disclosed.

#### 1. Introduction

[2] Carbon accounting is the process of measuring and reporting the greenhouse gases that an organisation is responsible for. Although it sounds like a technical exercise, it has become highly influential. Investors use footprint figures to assess climate risk, governments use them to design policy, and organisations use them to make public commitments such as becoming carbon neutral by a certain date. Yet unlike financial accounting, where a single set of rules usually applies within a country, carbon accounting allows organisations a number of legitimate choices. Standards for carbon reporting do exist, and they are widely used, but they generally set out acceptable approaches rather than requiring every organisation to calculate its footprint in exactly the same way. This flexibility helps organisations of very different types to report at all, but it also creates room for variation.

[3] Emissions are commonly divided into three categories, known as scopes. Scope 1 covers direct emissions from sources an organisation owns or controls, such as fuel burned in its vehicles or boilers. Scope 2 covers indirect emissions from the electricity, heat or steam it buys. Scope 3 covers all other indirect emissions in its value chain, both upstream and downstream, including the goods and services it purchases, business travel, employee commuting, and the use and disposal of the products it sells. For many organisations, scope 3 is by far the largest category, but it is also the hardest to measure, because it depends on information from suppliers and customers. Organisations often have to rely on estimates, such as average emissions for a type of product, rather than measured data, which adds uncertainty to their figures. Two organisations with identical activities could therefore report different scope 3 figures simply because they used different sources of estimates.

[4] Two choices are of particular interest in this study. The first concerns scope 2. Under the location-based method, an organisation calculates electricity emissions using the average emissions of the electricity grid in its region. Under the market-based method, it can instead reflect the specific electricity contracts it has chosen, for example a contract to buy electricity certified as coming from renewable sources. The second choice concerns scope 3: organisations must decide which of its many categories are relevant and how to estimate them, and reporting practices vary widely. Some organisations report only a few scope 3 categories, while others attempt a comprehensive assessment. Because scope 3 can be so large, these decisions may have a greater effect on the final figure than any other.

[5] Previous discussion of these issues has often been theoretical. This study aimed to measure how much these choices matter in practice by applying different methods to the same organisations. In particular, we wanted to know how large the differences between methods could be, whether they affected some sectors more than others, and whether they could change how organisations compared with one another.

#### 2. Method

[6] We constructed detailed operating data for twelve fictional organisations of a similar size, from four sectors: higher education, retail, manufacturing and professional services. For each organisation, we calculated three versions of its footprint. Version A included scopes 1 and 2, with scope 2 calculated using the location-based method. Version B was identical except that scope 2 used the market-based method, taking into account renewable electricity contracts. Version C used the market-based method and also included the scope 3 categories that are commonly reported in each sector, such as purchased goods, business travel and commuting. Using fictional organisations allowed us to control every input precisely, so that any differences between versions could only come from the accounting choices themselves, not from errors or gaps in real-world data. The disadvantage of this approach is discussed in the Limitations section.

[7] To keep the comparison fair, all calculations used the same emission factors, which are the standard values used to convert an activity, such as a kilowatt-hour of electricity or a kilometre of air travel, into an amount of greenhouse gas. We then compared the totals produced by each version, and examined how the ranking of the organisations, from lowest to highest footprint, changed between versions.

#### 3. Findings

[8] The first finding concerns scope 2. Organisations that had signed renewable electricity contracts reported substantially lower totals under Version B than under Version A. For two organisations, both in professional services, the change was large enough that their combined scope 1 and 2 emissions appeared to fall by more than half, even though their actual electricity use and the physical electricity grid were unchanged.

[9] The second and larger effect came from scope 3. When the commonly reported scope 3 categories were added in Version C, every organisation's footprint increased, but by very different amounts. In manufacturing and retail, purchased goods made scope 3 several times larger than scopes 1 and 2 combined. In professional services, business travel was the dominant scope 3 category. In higher education, commuting by staff and students formed a surprisingly large share, reflecting the large numbers of people travelling to campus each day.

[10] Third, the ranking of the organisations changed considerably between versions. An organisation that appeared to have one of the lowest footprints under Version B could appear among the highest under Version C. In other words, which organisations looked like climate leaders depended to a large extent on which method was chosen.

[11] Finally, we found that the choices most favourable to an organisation were not always the most informative. Version B produced the lowest figures for most organisations, but it said little about the emissions in their supply chains, which in several cases were the main source of their climate impact.

#### 4. Discussion

[12] These results suggest that a single footprint figure, presented without explanation, can be misleading. This is not because any of the methods we used is incorrect. Each is widely accepted and answers a slightly different question. The location-based method describes the emissions associated with the electricity an organisation physically uses in its region. The market-based method describes the effect of its purchasing decisions. Including scope 3 describes its wider influence on emissions through its value chain. Problems arise not from the methods themselves but when a figure calculated with one method is read as though it answered a different question, for example when a market-based total is taken as a description of physical emissions.

[13] The market-based method deserves particular attention. Supporters argue that it rewards organisations for choosing renewable electricity and so encourages investment in clean energy. Critics respond that, in some markets, buying certificates may not lead to any additional renewable generation, so an organisation's reported emissions can fall without any change in what actually happens in the atmosphere. Our study cannot settle this debate, because it depends on how electricity markets operate in each country. It does, however, show that the choice of method can change the reported picture dramatically. For readers, the practical lesson is that a reduction in reported emissions may reflect a change in purchasing contracts rather than a change in energy use, and the two should not be confused. Both may be worthwhile, but they are different kinds of achievement.

[14] There is also a risk of selective reporting. If organisations can choose among legitimate methods, they may be tempted to emphasise the figures that present them most favourably. This does not necessarily involve dishonesty; organisations may genuinely believe that their chosen method is the most appropriate. But it does mean that readers, including investors and journalists, need to look beyond the headline number. Clear disclosure of methods, including which choices were made and why, is therefore as important as the figures themselves.

#### 5. Limitations

[15] Because the organisations in this study were fictional, the results illustrate the size of possible effects rather than measuring how often they occur in real reporting. In addition, we included only commonly reported scope 3 categories; a complete scope 3 assessment might produce even larger differences. Finally, we used a single set of emission factors; in practice, organisations may also use different factors, which would add further variation.

#### 6. Conclusion and recommendations

[16] Carbon footprints are valuable tools, but they are constructed figures rather than simple facts. We recommend that organisations report both location-based and market-based scope 2 figures, state clearly which scope 3 categories they have included and why, and avoid comparing their footprint with those of other organisations unless the methods are the same. For readers, the most important question to ask about any footprint figure is not only how large it is, but how it was calculated. Asking that question will not always reveal a single correct answer, but it will make clear what a figure can and cannot tell us.

## L5-R2 · Critical review: Heritage languages at home

L5-07 · critical review · T505 Heritage language maintenance in migrant families · critical review

> **Check before release:** Book, author and reviewer are fictional. The general idea that heritage languages are often lost over about three generations is a widely discussed pattern in sociolinguistics; keep it general.

### Review of 'Speaking at Home: Language, Family and Belonging in Migrant Households' (fictional book)

#### Overview

[1] Why do some children of migrants grow up speaking their parents' language fluently, while others, raised in apparently similar circumstances, understand it only partly or not at all? This question lies at the heart of Speaking at Home, a new book by the sociolinguist Dr Mara Lindqvist. Drawing on interviews with forty migrant families over five years, Lindqvist argues that heritage language maintenance depends less on parents' determination than on the networks, institutions and attitudes that surround a family. It is an engaging and often moving book, and an important contribution to a field that has tended to focus on policy rather than on everyday family life. However, its central argument is stronger in some chapters than in others, and its methods raise questions that readers should keep in mind. This review first summarises the book's argument, then considers its main strengths and weaknesses. It concludes with an overall assessment of who is likely to find the book most useful.

#### Summary of the argument

[2] A heritage language is the language of a person's family or community, learned at home, which differs from the main language of the wider society. Researchers have long observed a pattern in which such languages are often lost within about three generations: migrants speak their first language fluently, their children become bilingual but increasingly prefer the majority language, and their grandchildren may speak only the majority language. Lindqvist accepts that this pattern is common, but she challenges the idea that it is inevitable, or that it is mainly the result of parents' choices. Instead, she suggests that the pattern reflects the conditions in which families live, and that different conditions can produce different outcomes, even within the same community. In this sense, the book is as much about society as it is about families: it asks what kind of environment makes it possible for a child to keep two languages.

[3] Her central claim is that families do not maintain languages alone. Lindqvist identifies three conditions that, in her data, distinguished families in which children remained confident speakers of the heritage language. The first was a network of other speakers, particularly grandparents and children of the same age, which gave the language a real purpose beyond communicating with parents. The second was institutional support, such as weekend language schools, community organisations and, in a few cases, bilingual programmes in mainstream schools. The third, and in her view the most important, was the status of the language: whether children saw it as valuable and modern or as old-fashioned and embarrassing. Lindqvist argues that when all three conditions were present, children usually remained confident speakers, and when none were present, even highly committed parents struggled.

[4] The book's middle chapters explore each of these conditions through detailed family stories. One of the most memorable describes two sisters, raised in the same household, who developed very different relationships with their parents' language. The elder, who spent several long holidays with her grandparents, became a fluent speaker; the younger, who did not, increasingly answered her parents in the majority language. For Lindqvist, this case shows that language maintenance is shaped by experience and opportunity rather than by family policy alone, since the parents' approach was the same for both children. Other chapters describe families who moved between neighbourhoods and found that their children's use of the language rose or fell with the number of other speakers nearby. Taken together, these stories build a picture of language maintenance as something that families negotiate continuously, rather than decide once. Lindqvist is careful to show that the same family can experience periods of strong and weak language use at different stages of their children's lives.

[5] The final chapters turn to recommendations. Lindqvist argues that governments and schools should treat heritage languages as a resource rather than a problem, for example by recognising them in school qualifications and supporting community language schools with funding and teacher training. She also suggests that teachers can make a difference simply by showing interest in their students' home languages, which may help to raise the status of those languages in children's eyes. These recommendations follow naturally from her argument, since each is designed to strengthen one of the three conditions she identifies.

#### Strengths

[6] The book's greatest strength is its attention to children's own perspectives. Much previous research has relied on parents' reports of how their children use language. Lindqvist interviewed children directly, at several points over five years, and their voices give the book its depth. We hear, for instance, a teenage boy explaining that he stopped speaking his family's language at school after classmates laughed at him, and later began using it again when a popular musician from his parents' country of origin became well known. Examples like this give real weight to her argument about status: they show how quickly a child's attitude can change when the wider social meaning of a language changes. The book is also unusually well written for an academic study: Lindqvist explains technical concepts clearly and lets her participants speak in their own words, which makes the book accessible to readers outside the field. Teachers in particular are likely to find much here that connects with their own experience of multilingual classrooms.

[7] A second strength is the book's balanced treatment of parents. Popular advice often implies that children lose a language because parents did not try hard enough. Lindqvist rejects this view firmly, and with evidence. Several of the families who were most committed to speaking the heritage language at home, and who had strict rules about its use, nonetheless saw their children move away from it. By shifting attention from individual effort to social conditions, the book is likely to be reassuring for many migrant parents, and useful for teachers and policymakers. It also offers a more realistic picture of family life, in which language decisions are made under pressure of time, work and children's own preferences, rather than according to a single plan. In this respect, the book corrects a tendency in some earlier work to present language choices as simple decisions that parents make once and then carry out.

#### Weaknesses

[8] The most significant weakness concerns the relationship between Lindqvist's evidence and her conclusions. Her sample of forty families is large for an interview study, but it was not designed to represent migrant families in general. The families were recruited mainly through community language schools, which means that most of them already had at least one of the institutional supports she identifies as important. As a result, the book can tell us a great deal about how language maintenance works in families that are already connected to community institutions, but much less about families who are not. This matters, because those less-connected families may be precisely the ones for whom her recommendations are most relevant. Lindqvist mentions this limitation briefly in her introduction, but she does not return to it when drawing her conclusions, which gives some of her claims a broader appearance than her evidence supports. A more cautious framing, limited to the kinds of families she actually studied, would have strengthened rather than weakened the book.

[9] A second concern is that the three conditions are not always clearly separated. Grandparents, for example, are discussed as part of a speaker network, but holidays with grandparents also seem to raise a language's status in children's eyes. Lindqvist acknowledges that the conditions overlap, but she nonetheless ranks status as the most important without explaining how she reached that judgement. A reader may accept that all three conditions matter while remaining unconvinced that one can be ranked above the others on the basis of this evidence. A study designed to compare families with and without each condition would be needed to test such a ranking properly. Without such a comparison, the ranking remains an interesting hypothesis rather than a finding. This is not a minor point, since the ranking shapes several of her recommendations.

[10] Finally, the policy chapters are less developed than the rest of the book. Lindqvist's recommendations are reasonable, but she says little about cost, about how schools would support many different heritage languages at once, or about evidence that similar policies have worked elsewhere. These chapters read more as a statement of values than as a fully argued policy case. Readers looking for practical guidance on implementing such policies will need to look elsewhere. It is possible that Lindqvist intended these chapters mainly to start a discussion, but if so, this could have been made clearer. Nevertheless, they raise the right questions for policymakers, even if they do not yet answer them.

#### Conclusion

[11] Speaking at Home is a thoughtful and humane book that makes a convincing case for one important claim: that the loss of heritage languages is not simply the result of parents' choices, and should not be blamed on them. Its detailed family stories, and especially its attention to children's voices, make it valuable reading for teachers, researchers and families alike. Its broader claims, however, particularly the ranking of status above the other conditions and the policy recommendations, would need further research, including families outside community networks, before they could be accepted with confidence. Read as an exploration rather than as a final answer, it succeeds very well. It also raises questions that future researchers would do well to take up.
